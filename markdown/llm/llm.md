Any new technology comes with a period of excitement and adjustment, and LLMs — commonly referred to as AI — are no exception. Given the hype and ease of access over the past couple of years, there are a few things worth reflecting on and keeping in mind going forward.

### LLM Basics

Without going too deep, a Large Language Model (LLM) is a neural network trained on a massive amount of text, enabling it to understand and generate language. Think of it as a very sophisticated autocomplete — one that has essentially read the entire internet and can hold a conversation on almost any topic.

A simpler way to picture it: imagine training a program to do one task, like getting a ball into a net. After a few iterations it starts to figure things out on its own, building on what worked before. LLMs do something similar, just at a scale that is hard to fully wrap your head around.

The architecture that made all of this possible is called a Transformer. Without getting too into the weeds, Transformers are good at figuring out which parts of a sentence (or paragraph, or document) are relevant to each other. That ability to weigh context is what separates modern LLMs from older approaches.

---

## The Good

Honestly, a lot of it is genuinely great. The speed at which you can go from an idea to a working first draft — whether that is code, writing, research, or planning — is hard to overstate. Tasks that used to take a few hours can get done in minutes, not because the quality is worse but because the boring scaffolding is handled for you.

For developers in particular, having something that can read an error message and suggest a fix, or explain an unfamiliar codebase, is a real change in how work gets done. The same goes for anyone doing research. You can have a long, specific conversation with something that has broad knowledge and get pointed in useful directions fast.

It also levels the playing field in some ways. You do not need to know how to code to build a basic tool anymore. You do not need a copywriter to draft a first version of something. Whether that is good or bad for certain industries is a separate conversation, but the access is real.

---

## The Bad

The problems start when people treat the output as ground truth. LLMs are not search engines and they are not databases. They generate text that sounds correct and confident regardless of whether it actually is. This is usually called hallucination, which is a polite way of saying the model just made something up.

The bigger issue is that it is not always obvious when this is happening. The answer reads the same whether it is accurate or completely fabricated. If you are not already familiar enough with the topic to spot a wrong answer, you are in a tough spot.

There is also the question of what these models are actually good at. They are trained on patterns in text, which means they are good at pattern matching — not reasoning, not logic, not math. They can look like they are doing those things, and sometimes they get the right answer, but the process underneath is fundamentally different from how a human works through a problem.

A lot of what is driving the pace of all this is not usefulness — it is speculation. The companies building these models are valued not on what they are earning today but on what investors believe they could be worth in five years. That creates a strong incentive to ship fast, hype loudly, and worry about the details later. When a company is valued in the hundreds of billions based largely on projected future dominance, slowing down to get things right is not always the move that gets rewarded. The people funding this space are making a bet, and that bet shapes every decision about what gets built, how fast, and with how much caution.

---

## The Ugly

The uncomfortable part is really about where the lines are and who gets to draw them. The training data that made these models useful came from real people — writers, developers, artists — who did not sign off on that. The energy and water costs of running these systems at scale are significant and mostly invisible to the people using them. And the speed at which this technology has been deployed means that the social and economic effects are already happening well before anyone has figured out how to handle them responsibly.

There are also cases that are harder to talk around. AI chatbots have been linked to real-world harm — teenagers in mental health crises who were encouraged deeper into suicidal ideation by a chatbot that had no real guardrails, just a very convincing conversational interface. There have been documented cases, lawsuits, and enough evidence at this point that dismissing it as edge cases is not really honest. The same applies to radicalization. A model trained to keep you engaged and validate your worldview is not neutral — it can quietly steer someone already struggling toward something much darker. The gap between what these companies test for and what actually happens when a vulnerable person sits down with one of these tools is significant, and most of the industry would rather not dwell on it.

There is also the question of dependency. The more that writing, thinking, and decision-making get offloaded to these tools, the harder it becomes to do those things without them. Whether that is a problem or just an evolution depends on who you ask, but it is worth being aware of.

None of this means the technology is bad or that you should not use it. It just means you should use it with a realistic picture of what it is, what it is not, and what the tradeoffs look like. The hype cycle makes that harder than it should be.

---

At the end of the day, LLMs are a tool. A weird, powerful, occasionally unreliable tool that is changing a lot of things very quickly. The best way to engage with it is probably the same as any other tool — learn what it is actually good at, stay skeptical, and do not let the marketing do your thinking for you.
